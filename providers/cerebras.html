Cerebras: CS-4 (three WSE-3 Turbo wafers)

Cerebras doesn't cut a wafer into chips. It keeps the whole wafer as one processor, with its memory on the same silicon. CS-4, announced 18 August 2026, puts three of these wafers in one rack, built for fast token generation. First shipments were due in Q3 2026.

Rack infrastructure

Power shelves

×3 at the front of each Nexus rack PRESS

Why it's special: a GPU board sends power through about 50 mm of circuit board before it reaches the chip. Cerebras says CS-4 converts power about 0.5 mm from the wafer, 100× closer, with no circuit board in the final path. That lets it feed the wafer twice the power it got before, which is how the same silicon runs faster.

Wafer-to-wafer network

Direct Wafer Links

Proprietary · within and across racks · no switch in between

Why it's special: GPU racks need switch chips to join their processors. Cerebras wires wafers straight to each other, so a message takes as little as 2 µs. That matters because a model too big for one wafer is split across several, and every token has to cross the links.

Key numbers

What it does here

  • Carries activations between wafers when one model is pipelined across several.
  • The Register reports a 2D torus layout with no mandatory switches PRESS.
  • Standard Ethernet (RoCE v2) is also supported, with possibly higher latency PRESS.

Accelerator

WSE-3 Turbo

×3 per system · 4 trillion transistors · TSMC 5nm PRESS

Why it's special: it's one processor the size of a wafer, about 57× the area of Nvidia's 814 mm² H100 die (derived). Turbo keeps the same 900,000 cores and the same silicon design as WSE-3. It gets twice the power and, per Cerebras, twice the speed. The Register estimates the clock went from about 1.4 to 2.8 GHz PRESS.

Cluster fabric (CS-3 era)

MemoryX + SwarmX

Documented for CS-3 · no CS-4 source mentions them

Why it's special: for training, CS-3 keeps a model's weights off the wafer in a MemoryX box and streams them in layer by layer, while SwarmX spreads them across many systems. The result: model size is limited by MemoryX, not by 44 GB of SRAM. For CS-4 inference, Cerebras instead describes pipelining a model across wafers. Whether MemoryX and SwarmX are used with CS-4 isn't stated.

Key numbers (CS-3)

1,200 TBMemoryX, largest option
2,048CS-3 systems linked by SwarmX, up to
~23 kWone CS-3 at peak

How big models fit

  • Streaming: weights live off-wafer, activations stay on it.
  • Pipelining (CS-4 inference): layers sit on different wafers. Heavy traffic stays inside each wafer, and mostly activations cross between wafers.

Concept

Power delivery close to the chip

Chips run on low voltage and huge current. Every millimetre that current travels wastes some of it as heat and lets the voltage sag. So the closer the power converter sits to the chip, the less is lost.

A wafer-sized chip makes this harder: power can't just arrive at the edge, because the middle would be starved. It has to come in from behind, across the whole face.

Analogy: watering a lawn. A hose at one corner leaves the far side dry; a sprinkler over every patch doesn't.
Concept

Liquid cooling

Almost all the power a chip uses becomes heat. Water carries far more heat than air, so direct liquid cooling pumps water through a metal cold plate pressed against the chip.

A wafer produces heat over a huge area, so Cerebras puts the water on one face of the wafer and the power on the other.

Analogy: a car radiator. Air alone can't cool a racing engine; a loop of liquid can.
Concept

Scale-out, with or without switches

Scale-out means joining many machines so they work on one model. Normally each machine plugs into a switch, a box that routes traffic. Switches add cost, power and a little delay.

A switch-free design wires machines directly to their neighbours. It saves the hop, but each machine must carry the routing itself.

Analogy: a roundabout versus a signalled junction. Neighbours pass straight through; nothing has to wait for a light.
Concept

Wafer-scale integration and the reticle limit

Chips are printed on a wafer, a thin disc of silicon, through a mask. One exposure covers a small area, the reticle limit, about 800 mm² or a bit more. Normally the wafer is then cut into many small chips. The biggest GPU dies sit at that limit.

Wafer-scale integration skips the cutting. Cerebras keeps a whole square of wafer, 46,225 mm², as one processor.

Analogy: a sheet of stamps. Everyone else tears it into single stamps; Cerebras uses the whole sheet as one stamp.
Concept

What are RoCE v2 and RDMA?

RDMA lets one machine write straight into another's memory without involving either CPU. RoCE v2 carries RDMA over ordinary Ethernet, so a system can talk to standard data-centre networks.

Analogy: couriers who drop a parcel on your desk, instead of handing it to reception to pass along.
Concept

What is a host CPU?

Accelerators can't run an operating system. A host CPU boots the machine, accepts requests, sets up the work and handles everything that isn't matrix maths.

Analogy: the receptionist who takes the call and passes it to the specialist.
Concept

Weight streaming and pipelining

A model's weights are its learned numbers. If they don't fit in a chip's memory, there are two ways out.

Weight streaming keeps weights in a separate store and feeds them to the chip layer by layer. Pipelining gives each chip its own layers and passes the in-progress results down the line.

Analogy: streaming is one cook fetching ingredients from a pantry as each step needs them. Pipelining is an assembly line, one station per step.
Concept

Prefill and decode

An AI answer has two phases. Prefill reads your whole prompt at once, which is heavy on maths. Decode then writes the answer one token at a time, and each token needs the whole model's weights read again, so memory speed is the limit.

Cerebras's on-wafer SRAM targets decode. That is why it pitches CS-4 alongside other chips doing prefill.

Analogy: reading a question carefully (prefill), then answering aloud one word at a time (decode).

Fabric

On-wafer 2D mesh fabric

Every core linked to its neighbours by short wires

Why it's special: cores pass data to neighbours over wires a fraction of a millimetre long, not over cables between chips. That's why one wafer can show 53.5 PB/s of fabric bandwidth, and why Cerebras says it needs no cable bundles to join its processors.

Key numbers

53.5 PB/son-wafer fabric, WSE-3 Turbo
27 PB/sWSE-3

What it does

  • Moves data between cores as the model's layers are laid out across the wafer.
  • Reroutes around dead cores (see "Spare cores").
  • "2D mesh" is Cerebras's long-standing description. The CS-4 sources checked here don't repeat it. The Register reports a 2D torus between wafers PRESS.

Defect tolerance

Spare cores and rerouting

970,000 cores built · 900,000 switched on (WSE-3)

Why it's special: a flaw anywhere on a wafer is certain, so the design expects them. When a core is bad, the fabric routes around it and a spare takes its place. Cerebras says a flaw costs it about 100× less silicon than it would cost a GPU.

Key numbers (WSE-3, Cerebras's calculation)

~46expected defects on the wafer
~2.2 mm²silicon lost (72 H100-size dies: ~361 mm²)
~7%cores not enabled (derived: 70,000 of 970,000)

What it does

  • Assumes about 0.001 defects per mm² on TSMC 5nm, a reported figure the blog cites.
  • Cerebras's own spare-core counts for WSE-3 Turbo aren't published.
  • These are company claims; no independent yield figure is public.

Power and cooling

Power and cooling delivery

Power through one face, water through the other

Why it's special: a wafer this big can't be fed from its edges, so power comes from behind and cooling from the front. CS-4 puts power conversion about 0.5 mm away, with no circuit board in the final path. That's what lets Turbo draw twice the power.

Key numbers

Concept

SRAM versus HBM

SRAM is memory built from the same transistors as the processor. It's extremely fast but takes lots of space, so a chip holds little of it. HBM is stacked DRAM beside the chip: slower, but far more of it.

A Rubin GPU has 288 GB of HBM4. One Cerebras wafer has 44 GB of SRAM. Cerebras wins on speed, loses on capacity. An AI core is one tiny processor, and the wafer has 900,000 of them.

Analogy: SRAM is the desk in front of you; HBM is a warehouse across the road.
Concept

Memory bandwidth

Bandwidth is how much data memory can deliver per second. To write each token, a chip must read the model's weights, so bandwidth sets how fast tokens come out.

A Rubin GPU has about 22 TB/s. Cerebras quotes 43.2 PB/s per wafer, nearly 2,000× more (derived). The Register thinks real workloads won't reach the peak.

Analogy: the width of a pipe. A wider pipe fills the bucket faster, however big the pump.
Concept

2D mesh

In a 2D mesh, processors sit on a grid and each connects to its north, south, east and west neighbours. Data hops from core to core. A router at each core decides which way it goes.

Short wires mean low delay and low power. The cost is that distant cores take several hops.

Analogy: a city street grid. Every block connects to the next, and there's more than one way across town.
Concept

Scribe lines and the reticle limit

Chip factories print the same pattern many times across a wafer. Each print covers a rectangle up to the reticle limit. Between the rectangles run blank strips, scribe lines, where a saw cuts the wafer into chips.

Cerebras doesn't cut. It adds wires across the strips instead.

Analogy: a tiled floor. Others cut along the grout lines to sell single tiles; Cerebras lays bridges across the grout and keeps the floor.
Concept

Yield and defect tolerance

Chip making leaves random flaws. A big chip is more likely to catch one, and a chip with a flaw is normally thrown away. The share that works is the yield.

GPU makers disable a few units to save flawed chips. Cerebras goes further: with tiny cores, a flaw kills only one core, and spares and rerouting cover for it.

Analogy: a pothole in one lane. A single-lane road is blocked; a street grid just goes round.
Concept

Edge I/O

I/O is how a chip talks to the outside. On a wafer, the interior is packed with cores, so the links to other systems come off the edges.

Because edge links are few compared with the fabric inside, the wafer's design keeps most traffic local.

Analogy: a walled town. Streets inside are wide and busy; only a few gates lead out.
Concept

Vertical power and cooling

Edge-fed power would starve the middle of a wafer, so power arrives through the back face and a liquid-cooled plate carries heat away from the front. Parts that expand differently when hot must still stay connected.

Analogy: a sandwich. Power is the bottom slice, the cooler is the top, and the wafer is the filling that must stay in contact with both.

What's next

At Hot Chips 2026 Cerebras named a CS-5 for 2027, aiming at up to 10,000 tokens/s per user on open models, and a CS-6 that adds 3D-stacked DRAM to the wafer's SRAM. It hasn't published specs, and it doesn't use the name "WSE-4". It also says Nexus racks will carry several generations, and a roadmap of roughly 2× a year through 2029 is reported PRESS.

The business

Cerebras listed on Nasdaq (CBRS) on 13 May 2026, pricing at $185 a share and raising about $5.55 billion at a reported valuation near $56.4 billion PRESS. In January 2026 it signed a deal to supply OpenAI with up to 750 MW of inference capacity, reported as worth over $10 billion, with later reports of expansion to 2 GW PRESS. Its filings, per press analysis, show heavy dependence on a few customers PRESS.
